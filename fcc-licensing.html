Do You Need an FCC License for Your Two-Way Radios?

A plain-language guide to when a license is required, when it isn’t, and how businesses in Florida can stay compliant.

Two-way radios keep teams connected when phones aren’t practical. Because radio spectrum is shared and regulated, some systems need a Federal Communications Commission (FCC) license — and some don’t. The difference depends on the radio service, how the radios are programmed, and whether you’re using them for personal or business operations.

Quick answer

License usually required

  • Business / industrial systems (often FCC Part 90)
  • Radios programmed for dedicated frequencies to limit interference
  • GMRS (personal licensed service)

Individual license usually not required

  • FRS (Family Radio Service)
  • MURS (Multi-Use Radio Service)
  • CB (Citizens Band)

Still must use authorized gear and follow that service’s rules.

Why the FCC licenses some radios

Licensing is mainly about controlling interference. When too many users transmit on the same channels without coordination, audio becomes unreliable. For schools, plants, fleets, security, and public-facing operations, that isn’t just an inconvenience — it can affect safety and response.

A license ties operation to an area, technical parameters, and (for business systems) frequency assignments so networks can scale without stepping on each other.

What typically needs a license — and what doesn’t

Radios can look identical on the shelf. The rules depend on the service and how the equipment is used.

Usually licensed

  • Business and industrial systems — facilities, manufacturing, logistics, campuses, municipalities, multi-site teams (commonly under Part 90).
  • GMRS — a personal radio service that requires an individual FCC license.

Often license-by-rule (no individual license)

  • FRS — consumer handhelds under FRS rules
  • MURS — VHF channels under MURS rules
  • CB — 27 MHz service under CB rules

“License-by-rule” does not mean unregulated. Equipment must be authorized for that service, and power, channels, and operating practices still apply.

Common misconception: “walkie-talkie” ≠ always license-free

Buying a radio does not automatically make every use legal. Compliance depends on:

  • Which radio service the unit is built and authorized for
  • How channels are programmed
  • Whether the equipment is FCC-authorized for that service

That matters even more for organizations, where radios are often coded for private channels and shared across shifts and locations.

How to figure out what you have

  1. Read the label and manuals. Look for FRS, GMRS, MURS, or language that points to business / Part 90 use.
  2. Review how the radios were programmed. Consumer channel packs differ from organization-specific frequencies set up by a dealer.
  3. Confirm FCC authorization for the intended service. The right hardware on the wrong service (or the reverse) is a compliance problem.

If you inherited a system, or run radios across a plant, campus, or fleet, a short licensing review is cheaper than discovering a gap after interference or a renewal notice.

Business and industrial licensing (what most organizations need)

When radios support daily operations — not casual personal chat — you may need a business or industrial license, especially if you use repeaters, higher power, or multiple sites.

What the license is tied to

  • Service area and site locations
  • Assigned frequencies
  • Station types (portable, mobile, base, repeater)
  • Coverage goals and interference protection

Good times to review licensing

  • You took over an existing system and don’t have clear records
  • You’re adding users, buildings, or repeaters
  • You’re hearing interference or coverage has degraded
  • You received a renewal notice or a third-party solicitation and want to verify what’s legitimate

GMRS in brief (personal / family use)

General Mobile Radio Service is aimed at individuals and families. An FCC license is required; there is no exam, applications are filed through the FCC’s systems (commonly Form 605), and licenses are typically issued for a multi-year term to an individual, with family operation under the rules that apply.

Businesses should not assume GMRS is the right path for operational fleets — Part 90 business licensing is usually the correct framework for commercial systems.

How to get the right license

  1. Identify the service. Business/Part 90 vs GMRS vs license-by-rule (FRS, MURS, CB). Collect any existing call signs or paperwork if the system was inherited.
  2. Apply through official FCC channels. Most wireless applications and updates go through the FCC’s Universal Licensing System (ULS).
  3. Use FCC guidance for industrial/business systems. Start with the FCC’s materials on industrial and business licensing, then match frequency and equipment to your real coverage needs.
  4. Work with a local radio partner when helpful. Dealers familiar with Florida coordination and Part 90 systems can reduce guesswork on applications, renewals, and system design.

Official rules and forms live on fcc.gov. Avoid relying solely on unsolicited renewal letters — confirm call signs and status in ULS.
